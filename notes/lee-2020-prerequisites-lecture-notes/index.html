The one idea

Hwangbo 2019 trained one network by reinforcement learning on the observation the robot can actually measure, and it worked on flat ground.

On rough terrain the same recipe stalls. The paper reports the failure in one sentence (p. 3): trained directly by RL, the proprioceptive network did not learn to walk within a reasonable time budget because "the supervisory signal was sparse." The reading developed in §3 is that with proprioception alone the network has to solve two problems at once, what is the ground doing and what should the legs do, and a scalar reward per step is too little signal to learn both.

Lee 2020 separates the two problems.

  1. A teacher is trained by RL in simulation with the ground truth handed to it as input (terrain shape under each foot, contact states and forces, friction, external pushes). It only has to learn what to do.
  2. A student is trained by supervised imitation to reproduce the teacher's actions and the teacher's internal summary of the ground truth, from a two-second window of proprioceptive history. It only has to learn to infer what the ground is doing.
  3. A terrain curriculum chooses the terrains both are trained on. A particle filter tracks the set of terrains that are currently hard but passable, and follows that set as the policy improves.

Under all three sits the same motion machinery as before, plus one addition: the network does not output joint targets but modulates a fixed periodic foot trajectory, which is turned into joint targets by inverse kinematics and tracked by joint PD control through the actuator net.

The teacher never leaves the simulator

Everything unusual in this paper follows from one decision: training is split into a stage that is allowed to cheat and a stage that is not. Because the teacher will never run on hardware, its observation may contain anything the physics engine knows; its action can be evaluated at any state the student wanders into, which is what DAgger needs (§3); and the terrains it trains on can be scored by ground-truth success, which is what the curriculum needs (§5). The rule of Sensors vs Simulator in Robot RL still binds the student. It is the teacher that is exempt.

1 · Motion prior: phase, trajectory generator, horizontal frame, inverse kinematics

🔴 for the trajectory-generator idea and the phase variable · 🟡 for analytic inverse kinematics

p.8 · Why the network does not output joint targets

Hwangbo 2019's policy emitted twelve joint position targets and a fixed PD law turned them into torque (Position Actions as Impedance Control in Robot RL). Lee 2020 keeps the PD law and the actuator net but moves the network one level up: its output is a modulation of a hand-designed periodic motion, not the motion itself.

The problem this solves is exploration on rough terrain. A freshly initialised policy outputs noise. If noise means random joint targets, the robot falls within a second, every episode terminates early, and the reward carries almost no information about which direction of change was better. If instead the robot is already stepping in place when training begins, noise means slightly different steps, episodes last long enough to compare, and the reward becomes informative.

The supplement states the first half of this directly: with reasonably tuned base frequency, step height, and initial phases, "a quadruped can stably step in place" (S3). The paper states the second half when it explains the output frame: defining the output in the horizontal frame "results in less premature termination at the beginning of the policy training, when the base motion is unstable due to random actions" (p. 8).

The design pattern is called Policies Modulating Trajectory Generators, PMTG (Iscen et al. 2018). A trajectory generator produces a nominal periodic motion from a phase. The policy outputs two things: parameters that change how the generator runs, and a residual that is added to the generator's output. The target sent downstream is generator output plus residual. The generator is a prior, not a constraint: if the policy learns a large residual, it can override the nominal motion entirely. What the prior buys is a sensible starting point and a coordinate system in which "lift the foot a little more" is a small number.

A network fed the raw angle sees two nearly identical situations as maximally different inputs, and has to learn that 000 and 2π2\pi2π mean the same thing.

The pair ⟨cos⁡ϕ,sin⁡ϕ⟩\langle\cos\phi, \sin\phi\rangle⟨cosϕ,sinϕ⟩ is continuous in ϕ\phiϕ and one-to-one onto the unit circle, so the same leg state always produces the same input and neighbouring leg states produce neighbouring inputs. This is the standard way to hand an angle to a network; the paper's phrase for it is "a smooth and unique representation for the angle."

Each cubic is a Hermite segment with zero slope at both ends, so the foot leaves the ground and lands with zero vertical velocity. That is the same reason the swing-foot planners in model-based stacks use splines with zero end velocity: a soft touchdown does not depend on knowing exactly where the ground is. The paper notes that a sine would also do; any smooth bump works as a prior.

The generator has no horizontal component. The nominal motion is stepping in place, directly under the hip. All forward progress comes from the residuals. The policy is not correcting a walking motion; it is turning a stepping motion into walking by adding lateral offsets that the generator knows nothing about. This is why the residuals are 12-dimensional and the frequencies are the only other output: the generator supplies timing and clearance, the policy supplies placement.

p.8 · The horizontal frame

The generator's output and the residuals are expressed in a frame HiH_iHi​ attached under the hip of leg iii at the nominal reach. Its zzz-axis is parallel to the gravity vector ege_geg​ and its xxx-axis is the projection of the base's xxx-axis onto the horizontal plane. So HiH_iHi​ turns with the robot's yaw but ignores its roll and pitch (p. 8, from Barasuol et al. 2013).

The nominal reach is the leg's hip-to-foot distance in the default standing configuration, not the fully extended length, so the origin of HiH_iHi​ sits at the default foothold of leg iii: a zero residual leaves the foot where it rests in stance. The origin, though, does none of the work this section credits to the frame. Only the orientation gives the level prior, and a constant translation of the origin is indistinguishable from a constant bias in the residual, which training absorbs; the inverse kinematics needs the hip's position in the base frame in any case. What the choice buys is an action space in which zero means neutral and, because the frame is per-leg, the same residual means the same displacement on all four legs.

Why this matters is easiest to see by imagining the alternative. If foot targets were expressed in the base frame, a pitched base would drag the nominal stepping motion with it: the generator would push feet into the ground on one side and lift them into the air on the other whenever the body tilted, and the policy would have to cancel the tilt with residuals before it could do anything else.

In HiH_iHi​ the prior stays level no matter what the base does, so the residuals only have to handle terrain. The paper's summary is that the frame "reduces the effect of base attitude on the foot motions and consequently stabilizes training" (p. 8).

You have used this frame under another name. The convex MPC of Di Carlo 2018 and the foothold planner in your article both rotate by yaw only, Rz(ψ)R_z(\psi)Rz​(ψ), and treat roll and pitch as disturbances to be corrected rather than as part of the reference geometry (Convex MPC for Legged Locomotion). HiH_iHi​ is the same choice applied to foot targets.

Two consequences the paper draws from the frame.

  • Converting a target from HiH_iHi​ to the base frame requires the roll and pitch of the base, which is exactly the information the IMU's gravity vector provides (Sensors vs Simulator in Robot RL); yaw is not needed, and the IMU cannot give it anyway.
  • Because Hiz{}^{H_i}zHi​z is aligned with gravity, "lateral" and "vertical" are separate coordinates of the residual, and the stochastic policy's exploration noise can be shaped per direction: larger noise laterally, "to promote exploration along the ground surface" (p. 8).

That sentence is only meaningful once you know that the exploration noise of a Gaussian policy is a standard deviation per action coordinate, which is where §2 begins.

p.8 · Analytic inverse kinematics

Each target foot position is expressed in HiH_iHi​, rotated into the base frame, and converted to three joint angles by closed-form inverse kinematics. Those joint targets are then tracked by the joint PD controllers at 400 Hz400\ \mathrm{Hz}400 Hz (Fig. 4C), through the actuator net in simulation and the real actuators on the robot.

An ANYmal leg has three joints: hip abduction/adduction (HAA), hip flexion/extension (HFE), and knee flexion/extension (KFE). The inverse problem decouples into two planar problems. In the frontal plane, the lateral offset of the target fixes the HAA angle by a single arctangent. In the plane of the leg, the remaining two joints form a two-link chain, and the law of cosines gives the knee angle from the hip-to-foot distance ddd; the hip pitch follows from the direction of the target and the interior angle at the hip. Two configurations satisfy the same target, knee forward and knee backward, and the implementation fixes one.


Textbook version of the leg's inverse kinematics. Left: the hip roll angle from the frontal plane. Right: hip pitch and knee angle from the sagittal plane, with the second solution dashed. The real ANYmal leg has a few fixed offsets between joint axes that add terms but not ideas.

The paper gives two reasons for analytic rather than numerical inverse kinematics (p. 8): computational efficiency, since the tracking loop runs at 400 Hz400\ \mathrm{Hz}400 Hz on an onboard CPU, and "to reuse existing position control actuator models for the sim-to-real transfer." The second reason is the deeper one. The actuator net of Hwangbo 2019 was trained on position-control data: joint position error and velocity in, torque out. By ending in joint position targets, this paper reuses the entire tracking stack, the PD law and the learned actuator, without touching it. Whatever the network does upstream, the part of the system that had been validated against hardware stays as it was.

There is also a reason not to skip the foot-space detour and let the network output joint targets directly, and it is the same reason the prior exists at all. Write the leg's forward kinematics as pf=k(q)p_f = k(q)pf​=k(q), the foot position as a function of the three joint angles, and differentiate:

Δpf=J(q) Δq,J(q)=∂k∂q∈R3×3,soΔq=J(q)−1 Δpf.\Delta p_f = J(q)\, \Delta q, \qquad J(q) = \frac{\partial k}{\partial q} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \Delta q = J(q)^{-1}\, \Delta p_f . Δpf​=J(q)Δq,J(q)=∂q∂k​∈R3×3,soΔq=J(q)−1Δpf​.

Everything follows from the argument of JJJ: the request in foot space is a constant, the answer in joint space is configuration-dependent. "Lift the foot two centimetres" is Δpf=(0,0,0.02)\Delta p_f = (0, 0, 0.02)Δpf​=(0,0,0.02) whatever the leg happens to be doing, while the Δq\Delta qΔq that delivers it is a different triple of numbers for every qqq — small with the leg crouched, large near full extension, where JJJ is ill-conditioned in the vertical direction.

So there is no fixed map from phase to joint angles that means "a 20 cm20\ \mathrm{cm}20 cm swing": the same joint trajectory would trace a different foot path every time the posture changed. That is why eq. (11) can be one cubic in one coordinate, and why the per-direction exploration noise of p.8 · The horizontal frame is definable at all — joint space has no axis called lateral. The generator and the network work in the space where the geometry is trivial, and the conversion is charged to the inverse kinematics once per step.

p.8 · The MDP paragraph, and what off-the-shelf hides

The paper's MDP paragraph is the textbook definition (Markov Decision Process (MDP)): state space, action space, reward, transition probability, a policy π(at∣st)\pi(a_t \mid s_t)π(at​∣st​), and the discounted return over an infinite horizon. The one non-standard move is the state, st=⟨ot,xt⟩s_t = \langle o_t, x_t\ranglest​=⟨ot​,xt​⟩: the robot-measurable observation together with the privileged information. With xtx_txt​ included, the teacher's environment is treated as fully observable, and the paper then says it uses "an off-the-shelf RL method [36]" (p. 8), which is TRPO.

"Off-the-shelf" hides three choices that Table S7 exposes: how the policy is parameterised for continuous actions, how the step size is controlled, and how the constrained step is computed. Each is a prerequisite for reading that table.

Gaussian policies: exploration is a standard deviation

For a continuous action, the standard policy class is a Gaussian whose mean is the network output,

πθ(a∣s)=N(a; μθ(s), Σ),Σ=diag⁡(σ12,…,σ162).\pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \mathcal{N}\big(a;\ \mu_\theta(s),\ \Sigma\big), \qquad \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_{16}^2). πθ​(a∣s)=N(a; μθ​(s), Σ),Σ=diag(σ12​,…,σ162​).

The network computes μθ(s)\mu_\theta(s)μθ​(s), a 16-vector of frequencies and residuals. The standard deviations are separate parameters, learned or scheduled, one per action coordinate. Sampling a∼πθa \sim \pi_\thetaa∼πθ​ during training is the exploration; at deployment the mean is used and the controller is deterministic. The score function for the mean is ∇μlog⁡π=Σ−1(a−μ)\nabla_\mu \log \pi = \Sigma^{-1}(a - \mu)∇μ​logπ=Σ−1(a−μ), which is L7's Gaussian policy slide (Lecture 7 · Policy Gradient › p.17–18 · Softmax and Gaussian Policies ⏱ 36:49): actions that came out above the mean and turned out well push the mean up.

Now the sentence from §1 reads plainly. "We applied larger noise in the lateral direction" (p. 8) means σ\sigmaσ is larger for the xxx and yyy components of the residuals than for zzz. That is only a well-defined choice because the residuals live in HiH_iHi​, where zzz is along gravity. The stated aim is "to promote exploration along the ground surface" (p. 8): lateral exploration tries different footholds, which is where the terrain problem lives.

Why a step size in parameter space is the wrong knob

Vanilla policy gradient updates θ←θ+α g^\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\, \hat gθ←θ+αg^​ with a learning rate α\alphaα measured in parameter space. The problem is that the same ∥Δθ∥\lVert\Delta\theta\rVert∥Δθ∥ can be a negligible or a catastrophic change in behaviour, depending on where the policy currently is.

The Gaussian makes this concrete. Shift the mean of N(μ,σ2)\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)N(μ,σ2) by Δμ\Delta\muΔμ. The Kullback–Leibler divergence between old and new policy is

DKL(N(μ,σ2) ∥ N(μ+Δμ,σ2))=Δμ22σ2,D_{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\,\big\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mu + \Delta\mu, \sigma^2)\big) = \frac{\Delta\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}, DKL​(N(μ,σ2)​N(μ+Δμ,σ2))=2σ2Δμ2​,

so the same parameter step Δμ=0.5\Delta\mu = 0.5Δμ=0.5 is a divergence of 0.1250.1250.125 when σ=1\sigma = 1σ=1 and of 12.512.512.5 when σ=0.1\sigma = 0.1σ=0.1. In the second case almost every action the new policy samples would have been essentially impossible under the old one. As training narrows σ\sigmaσ, a fixed learning rate turns into ever larger policy changes, exactly when the policy has become good and has the most to lose.

Two things go wrong when a step changes the policy too much. The gradient estimate was computed from the old policy's data and is a local statement; far from θk\theta_kθk​ it says nothing, so the step may make things worse. And a locomotion policy that has been made worse generates worse data (short episodes, falls), from which recovery is slow. This is the failure people call performance collapse, and avoiding it is the entire motivation for measuring the step in the space of behaviours rather than of parameters.

What TRPO does not guarantee

The theorem bounds the true return using the maximum per-state KL; the algorithm constrains the mean. The theorem's penalty is replaced by a constraint whose δ\deltaδ is tuned. The Fisher matrix is estimated from the same batch as the gradient. Each of these is standard, and each is a place where the guarantee is no longer a guarantee. When a later paper says its clipping "approximates a trust region", the Paper Reading Roadmap already lists the paper that checks whether it does (Engstrom et al. 2020, Section 5).

3 · Privileged learning: teacher, student, and DAgger

🔴

p.3, p.6 · The problem: one scalar per step cannot teach two things

The paper reports (p. 3) that training the proprioceptive network directly by RL failed: "the supervisory signal was sparse and the presented network failed to learn locomotion within reasonable time budgets." The ablation in Fig. 5E–G shows the same thing under controlled conditions: a TCN-20 trained by TRPO on proprioception alone, with the teacher's reward and hyperparameters, never learns to balance (short episodes, low reward), while the same architecture trained by imitation of a privileged teacher does.

Read "sparse" as a statement about how much the signal says, not about how often it arrives; a reward arrives every step. This reading is mine, not the paper's, but it is what the ablation supports.

One scalar per step, mixing seven objectives, has to teach a network both what to extract from a two-second history (is a foot trapped? is the ground sloping? was I pushed?) and what to do about it. The two problems are coupled: the network cannot learn what to extract until it knows what matters for acting, and cannot learn to act well until it extracts the right things. Privileged learning breaks the coupling by solving the second problem first, with the answer to the first handed over as input.

The pattern comes from learning by cheating (Chen et al. 2019), where an agent with access to the ground-truth layout of a driving scene is trained first, and a second agent that sees only camera images is trained to imitate it. This paper's variation is that the privileged agent is trained by RL rather than from human demonstrations, because nobody can demonstrate ANYmal over rough terrain.

p.8 · Privileged information: what the teacher is allowed to know

Table S4 lists the privileged block xtx_txt​: the terrain normal at each foot, the elevation of nine scan points on a 10 cm10\ \mathrm{cm}10 cm circle around each foot, foot contact forces and states, thigh and shank contact states, the friction coefficient under each foot, and the external force applied to the base during training. Every one of these is a hidden cause of something the student will see in proprioception: a slipping foot, an unexpected stop of a descending leg, a base that accelerates without the legs having done anything.

The rule of Sensors vs Simulator in Robot RL is that an observation must be measurable on the robot. That rule was stated for a policy that deploys. The teacher does not deploy, so its observation may contain simulator-only quantities, exactly as the reward always could. The split between what is allowed in the reward and what is allowed in the observation has moved: for the teacher, both lists are the simulator's.

The teacher does not consume xtx_txt​ raw. An encoder compresses it into a 64-dimensional latent lˉt\bar l_tlˉt​ (Table S5: tanh⁡(72)→tanh⁡(64)\tanh(72) \to \tanh(64)tanh(72)→tanh(64)), and the action head takes lˉt\bar l_tlˉt​ together with oto_tot​. The command and the robot's own state are deliberately kept out of xtx_txt​, so that lˉt\bar l_tlˉt​ "contains only the terrain- and contact-related features" (p. 8). That bottleneck is a design choice made for the student's sake: it produces a compact, well-defined target for what the student's history must encode, "what the ground is doing", in 64 numbers.

p.9 · Behavior cloning is regression, and where it breaks

Imitation learning in its simplest form is supervised regression. Given a dataset D\mathcal{D}D of input–label pairs (z,a∗)(z, a^*)(z,a∗), where a∗a^*a∗ is the expert's action at that input, fit a policy by minimising the squared error over the dataset. The paper's student loss is this, with two labels (eq. 1):

L=∥aˉt(ot,xt)−at(ot,H)∥2+∥lˉt(ot,xt)−lt(H)∥2.\mathcal{L} = \big\lVert \bar a_t(o_t, x_t) - a_t(o_t, H)\big\rVert^2 + \big\lVert \bar l_t(o_t, x_t) - l_t(H)\big\rVert^2 . L=​aˉt​(ot​,xt​)−at​(ot​,H)​2+​lˉt​(ot​,xt​)−lt​(H)​2.

Bars are the teacher's outputs, computed with the privileged xtx_txt​; unbarred are the student's, computed from the history HHH. Compared with RL, this is an easy problem for two reasons. The supervision is dense: eighty numbers per step, sixteen action components and sixty-four latent components, each with a definite target, instead of one scalar. And there is no credit assignment across time: each step is its own regression problem.

The catch is the distribution the regression is accurate on. A regressor is accurate on inputs like those it was trained on. If D\mathcal{D}D is collected by running the teacher, the student learns to be accurate on the states the teacher visits.

But the student is not the teacher. Its small errors move it to states slightly off the teacher's distribution, where it was trained less and errs more, which moves it further off, where it errs more still. This is covariate shift, and in sequential decision-making it compounds: Ross and Bagnell (2010) show that a per-step imitation error of ϵ\epsilonϵ on the training distribution can cost O(T2ϵ)O(T^2 \epsilon)O(T2ϵ) over a horizon of TTT steps, because one mistake can put the learner in unfamiliar states for the rest of the episode. Plain behavior cloning of a locomotion teacher would produce a student that walks well for a moment and then falls in ways the teacher never did.

p.9 · DAgger: label where the student goes

DAgger, dataset aggregation (Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell 2011), fixes the distribution by changing who generates the states. Iterate: run the current student and record the states it visits; ask the expert for its action at each of those states; add these pairs to the aggregate dataset; retrain the student on everything collected so far. The original algorithm mixes expert and student control in early iterations with a decaying probability βi\beta_iβi​; the essential step is that the labels are collected on the student's state distribution. Under a no-regret learner, the returned policy's cost is O(Tϵ)O(T\epsilon)O(Tϵ) plus regret terms rather than O(T2ϵ)O(T^2\epsilon)O(T2ϵ) (Ross et al. 2011, Section 3, Theorems 3.1–3.2).

The paper's description is one sentence and is exactly this: "training data is generated by rolling out trajectories by the student policy. For each visited state, the teacher policy computes its embedding and action vectors" (p. 9).

Notice what DAgger requires of the expert. It must answer "what would you do here?" at arbitrary states chosen by the student, not just at states on its own trajectories. A dataset of human demonstrations cannot do that. A teacher that runs in the simulator can, trivially: at whatever state the student has reached, the physics engine supplies xtx_txt​, the teacher network is evaluated, and the labels lˉt,aˉt\bar l_t, \bar a_tlˉt​,aˉt​ are written down. This is the second reason privileged learning and DAgger belong together, and it is another consequence of the tip in The one idea.

Once the student is trained on its own state distribution, there is no shift between training and test in simulation. The remaining shift is between simulation and the robot, and DAgger does nothing about that one. It is addressed, as in Hwangbo 2019, by randomising dynamics and friction, adding observation noise and pushes, and by the actuator net (S2).

4 · Memory: partial observability and the TCN

p.8–9 · Why the student needs a history

The student sees oto_tot​ and not xtx_txt​. Everything in xtx_txt​ still affects what happens next, so the student's observation is not a Markov state: the same oto_tot​ can occur with a foot about to hit a step or on flat ground, on ice or on rubber, pushed or not pushed.

In RL vocabulary the student faces a partially observable problem, a POMDP (Silver L1; the POMDP slides of L2 were skipped, but the aliased gridworld of Lecture 7 · Policy Gradient › p.7–9 · Aliased Gridworld ⏱ 15:25 is the same failure in miniature: identical observations in different situations). A policy that reacts to oto_tot​ alone must react identically in situations that call for different actions.

There are three standard cures.

  1. The exact one: the belief state, the posterior over the hidden variables given the whole history, which converts the POMDP into an MDP over beliefs; it is exact and intractable here.
  2. The second: feed a window of recent observations and let the network compute whatever statistic of it is useful.
  3. The third: a recurrent network that carries a state forward.

The paper takes the second cure with a window of N=100N = 100N=100 observations at 0.02 s0.02\ \mathrm{s}0.02 s, two seconds, and states its hypothesis on p. 8: the teacher's latent lˉt\bar l_tlˉt​ "can be (partially) recovered from a time series of proprioceptive observations." The word partially is honest: the history predicts what has been touched, not what lies ahead.

Why should hidden causes be recoverable from proprioception at all? Because they leave traces.

  • A foot that hits a step stops descending early: a jump in position error and a drop in velocity in that leg's joints.
  • A foot that slips moves while the leg is in stance.
  • A push changes the base velocity without any leg having done anything to cause it.
  • Terrain height is where the foot stopped.

Hwangbo 2019 already exploited this with a history of two samples, as its substitute for a contact sensor (Sensors vs Simulator in Robot RL › Contact detection without a force sensor); this paper extends the same idea to a hundred samples and lets the network decide what to extract.

How much history is needed is an empirical question, and the paper answers it in Fig. 5B–D. On a uniform slope, one sample is enough; the slope is visible in the gravity vector at every instant. On a step, memory decides: the hind legs have to clear a step that only the front legs have touched, a second or more earlier at walking speed, and short-memory controllers fail there. Under a lateral push, the two-second controller deviates about a third less than the one-sample controller.

Three quantities are dropped from the student's per-step input hth_tht​ (p. 9): the base frequency, the joint history, and the previous foot targets. All three were short-hand histories inside oto_tot​, and they are redundant once the window itself is the input.

The paper lists three assumptions (p. 9) and says where the first comes from and what the second and third are for.

wj=P(yil∣cT,jl)∑mP(yim∣cT,jm).w_j = \frac{P\big(y^l_i \mid c^l_{T,j}\big)}{\sum_m P\big(y^m_i \mid c^m_{T,j}\big)} . wj​=∑m​P(yim​∣cT,jm​)P(yil​∣cT,jl​)​.

The weight carries no particle index lll, and yyy carries the policy counter iii where eq. 6 writes yjky^k_jyjk​; read both as notation slips. What matters is the structure: the weight is recomputed each cycle from the current measurement probability alone, with no previous-weight factor. The normalisation is the sum in the denominator (self-normalisation).

Why that is consistent, which the paper does not explain: line 17 resamples in every cycle, so the particles entering the next weighting carry equal weights. Without resampling the weight would be the product wt∝wt−1 p(yt∣xt)w_t \propto w_{t-1}\, p(y_t \mid x_t)wt​∝wt−1​p(yt​∣xt​); after resampling at every step, wt−1=1/Nw_{t-1} = 1/Nwt−1​=1/N is absorbed into the proportionality, and the weight is the likelihood under the transition used as the proposal.

The lectures assume the likelihood is evaluated exactly at each particle. Here it is a random fraction, and two consequences have no lecture behind them.

  • The weights inherit the estimate's noise. If the policy were fixed over the window, the fraction would be an unbiased, non-negative estimate of Td\mathrm{Td}Td; independence of the trajectories matters for its variance, not its mean. Particle filters that put such estimates into the weights go by the name random-weight particle filters, and the lectures do not cover them.
  • A particle whose 60 trajectories all fall outside the band gets weight 000 and cannot survive resampling. Under the same independence assumption, a terrain with Td=0.02\mathrm{Td} = 0.02Td=0.02 would be estimated at 000 with probability 0.9860≈0.300.98^{60} \approx 0.300.9860≈0.30 (my arithmetic). If normalisation is per terrain type (not stated) and every particle of a type scores 000, the denominator of line 16 is 000; if it is pooled over all types, a type whose particles all score 000 loses them all at resampling, and the denominator is 000 only when every particle of every type scores 000. The paper addresses neither case.

Line 17 resamples NparticleN_{particle}Nparticle​ parameters, choosing the kkkth with probability equal to its normalised weight (p. 9). What you need is the categorical draw and its random copy counts: resampling draws from the weighted particle set, not from the target itself. It runs unconditionally; there is no effective-sample-size test. Its costs are the lectures' impoverishment (duplicated values after resampling) and deprivation (no particles left in a region that matters). On a discrete grid such as C\mathcal{C}C, duplicates are not a defect in themselves.

Lines 18 and 20 are the replay memory. Line 18 appends cT,jc_{T,j}cT,j​; since it follows the resampling at line 17, the natural reading is the resampled set. Line 18 comes before line 20 samples from the memory, so the memory already includes the current cycle. The paper's entire rationale is one sentence: replay memory is used "to prevent degeneration of the particle filter and to avoid catastrophic forgetting" (S2). It gives no mechanism, no memory capacity, and does not say whether a particle drawn from the memory is also moved at line 21.

Read against the lectures (my reading, not the paper's), a draw from the memory puts back a value the current weights did not select. That resembles the random-particle injection of L7 §5 with past particle values in place of random ones, and the lecture's conclusion about injection carries over: it lowers the risk of deprivation without removing it, and it changes the target. The paper also does not say which failure it calls "degeneration." Weight degeneracy accumulated over many cycles, as in L7 §2, cannot build up here, because line 16 keeps no weight history.

Lines 19 and 21 are the transition. On p. 9 each parameter of a sampling point is shifted to its adjacent value with a fixed probability ptransitionp_{transition}ptransition​; line 21 words the move per particle. Neither says whether coordinates move independently, which neighbour is chosen, how large a step is, or what happens at the bounds of C\mathcal{C}C.

Terrain parameters have no dynamics of their own. Nothing in the simulator changes a particle's cTc_TcT​ between cycles, and resampling only copies values already in the set. Lines 20–21 are therefore the only way a value not in the current set can enter it. A replay draw can only restore a value that was in an earlier set; only the random walk can produce a value never visited before. Without them the parameter is static, and repeated resampling of a static parameter under multinomial resampling ends at a single value with probability 111. The lecture warns that roughening adds noise that is not in the model and so changes the target; here, on my reading, there is no model to depart from, since the random walk is the transition model by definition. The paper gives no reason for choosing a random walk; it says only that the random walk satisfies assumption 3 (p. 9).

The order of steps differs from the lectures' SIR cycle only in where it starts. L7 §7 runs propagate, weight, normalise, estimate, resample; Algorithm S1 runs rollouts, weight and normalise, resample, transition. It is the same cycle entered at the weighting step, with the estimate replaced by using the particles as the next terrains.

What separates Algorithm S1 from the SIR filter of the lectures:

  • Target. A distribution of terrain parameters weighted by desirability for a policy that keeps changing, not a posterior over a hidden state.
  • Transition. A designed random walk plus replay draws, not a known model of how anything evolves.
  • Likelihood. Estimated from 60 trajectories pooled over a window in which the policy is updated ten times; it can be 000 for every particle.
  • Output. No estimate is formed; the particle set is used directly as the next training terrains.
  • Bookkeeping. 10 particles per terrain type (Table S3); whether each type is weighted and resampled as its own filter is not stated.

🟡 · This chapter is short because the paper explains its own experiments well; what it assumes is the vocabulary.

Fig. 5 · Ablation logic, seeds, and intervals

An ablation removes one component while holding the rest fixed and measures what is lost. Fig. 5 does this three times: history length NNN (B–D), privileged training versus direct RL (E–G), adaptive versus uniform terrains (H–J), each on three diagnostic tests, an omnidirectional slope, a discrete step, and a lateral push, with 100 trials per test.

Every model was trained five times with different random seeds and the error bars are 95 % confidence intervals. Two sources of randomness are folded into those bars: the trials, whose success rate is a binomial proportion with standard error at most 0.25/100=0.05\sqrt{0.25/100} = 0.050.25/100​=0.05, and the training runs themselves, whose outcome varies with initialisation and sampling. Five seeds is the field's convention and is small; the Roadmap's Section 5 (Paper Reading Roadmap) lists the paper that shows why single-seed results are not results. Read Fig. 5 by asking whether the intervals overlap, not by the height of the bars.

p.12 · The decoder probe: what the encoding contains, with an uncertainty

To see what the student's history encoding holds, the paper trains a decoder after the fact, with the policy frozen, from an intermediate layer of the TCN back to the privileged information xtx_txt​. Discrete targets (contact states) use a cross-entropy loss. Continuous targets use a Gaussian negative log-likelihood in which the decoder predicts both a mean and a standard deviation per component (eq. 8):

L=∑i[(xigt−μi)22σi2+log⁡σi].\mathcal{L} = \sum_i \left[\frac{\big(x_i^{gt} - \mu_i\big)^2}{2\sigma_i^2} + \log \sigma_i\right]. L=i∑​[2σi2​(xigt​−μi​)2​+logσi​].

This is heteroscedastic regression (Kendall and Gal 2017). The first term punishes an error more when the network claims to be certain; the second punishes claiming uncertainty. Per sample the loss is minimised at σi=∣xigt−μi∣\sigma_i = \lvert x_i^{gt} - \mu_i\rvertσi​=∣xigt​−μi​∣, so the predicted σ\sigmaσ learns to track the actual error, and it can be read as the encoding's uncertainty. That is what Fig. 6B and Fig. S2 plot: the estimated elevation under the front feet rises when a foot hits a step, its uncertainty grows, and the uncertainty stays elevated afterwards on rough ground, which the paper reads as the encoding carrying terrain roughness.


The two terms of the Gaussian negative log-likelihood pull in opposite directions and meet at σ=∣y−μ∣\sigma = \lvert y - \mu\rvertσ=∣y−μ∣.

One caution on reading a probe. That xtx_txt​ can be decoded from the features shows the information is present in them, not that the policy uses it. The saliency analysis is the paper's answer to the second question.

p.12 · Saliency

Eq. (9) defines the saliency of the iii-th past measurement as

Mi=∑j∈channels∣∂ (rf,T)z∂Hi,j∣,M_i = \sum_{j \in \text{channels}} \left\lvert \frac{\partial\, (r_{f,T})_z}{\partial H_{i,j}} \right\rvert , Mi​=j∈channels∑​​∂Hi,j​∂(rf,T​)z​​​,

the absolute sensitivity of the commanded foot height to each entry of the input window, summed over channels: one row of the input–output Jacobian in the sense of Matrix Calculus (Gradients) (Simonyan et al. 2013). Fig. 6C–D shows the saliency peaking at the moment the front foot hit the step and staying high through the following swing: the policy keeps attending to that one collision sample while it lifts the foot higher. Decoding showed the information was there; saliency shows it was used.

p.5 · Cost of transport

The paper's efficiency measure is the mechanical cost of transport,
