Q1. What exactly is bootstrapping?

Asked: "bootstrap, on-policy, off policy 이런 용어 좀 쉽고 명확히 이해시켜줘" — 2026-08-19 chat, right after reading Lecture 5 · Model-Free Control.

Definition: bootstrapping means the update target is built using the algorithm's own current estimate, instead of waiting for the real outcome.

V(St)←V(St)+α(targett−V(St))V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\big(\text{target}_t - V(S_t)\big) V(St​)←V(St​)+α(targett​−V(St​))

The difference between methods is entirely in what target is:

MC (no bootstrap):Gt=∑k=0T−t−1γkRt+k+1TD(0) (bootstrap):Rt+1+γV(St+1)\begin{aligned} \text{MC (no bootstrap)} &: G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1}\gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \\ \text{TD(0) (bootstrap)} &: R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) \end{aligned} MC (no bootstrap)TD(0) (bootstrap)​:Gt​=k=0∑T−t−1​γkRt+k+1​:Rt+1​+γV(St+1​)​

GtG_tGt​ is the actual return the episode produced — no VVV estimate appears inside it, so nothing can be wrong except sampling noise. TD(0)'s target replaces everything after the first reward with V(St+1)V(S_{t+1})V(St+1​), a guess that can itself be wrong. That is bootstrapping: fixing one guess using another guess, rather than waiting for ground truth.

Analogy

Checking a bank balance by taking yesterday's balance and adding today's transactions, instead of re-auditing the account from scratch. Fast, but if yesterday's number was already off, the error carries forward.

Why it matters for L5 specifically: everything in model-free control bootstraps off QQQ instead of VVV. Sarsa's target R+γQ(S′,A′)R+\gamma Q(S',A')R+γQ(S′,A′) and Q-learning's target R+γmax⁡a′Q(S′,a′)R+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(S',a')R+γmaxa′​Q(S′,a′) are both one-step bootstraps — same mechanism as TD(0), just on the state-action value. → L5 notes §5, §8

This restates L4 Q1 in the control setting — the concept doesn't change from prediction to control, only which function (VVV vs QQQ) gets bootstrapped. Full target-family comparison (MC / TD(0) / nnn-step / λ\lambdaλ-return) is in Model-Free Prediction.

Q2. On-policy vs. off-policy — what is actually different?

Asked: same session, immediately following Q1.

The one-line distinction: is the policy you're acting with the same as the policy you're learning about?

Behaviour policy (acting) Target policy (learning about)
On-policy π\piπ same π\piπ
Off-policy μ\muμ different π\piπ

Sarsa is on-policy by construction, not by convention. The update

Q(S,A)←Q(S,A)+α(R+γQ(S′,A′)−Q(S,A))Q(S,A) \leftarrow Q(S,A) + \alpha\big(R+\gamma Q(S',A') - Q(S,A)\big) Q(S,A)←Q(S,A)+α(R+γQ(S′,A′)−Q(S,A))

is a sample of the Bellman expectation equation for qπq_\piqπ​. That equation's right-hand side is an expectation over your own policy from S′S'S′ onward — so the only way Q(S′,A′)Q(S',A')Q(S′,A′) is a valid sample of it is if A′A'A′ is actually drawn from the same π\piπ you're evaluating. Break that and the target no longer estimates the thing it claims to. → L5 notes, p.22 question

Q-learning is off-policy: it splits the action actually executed from the action used inside the bootstrap.

Q(St,At)←Q(St,At)+α(Rt+1+γmax⁡a′Q(St+1,a′)−Q(St,At))Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha\Big(R_{t+1}+\gamma \max_{a'}Q(S_{t+1},a') - Q(S_t,A_t)\Big) Q(St​,At​)←Q(St​,At​)+α(Rt+1​+γa′max​Q(St+1​,a′)−Q(St​,At​))

At+1A_{t+1}At+1​ (what you actually do next) is drawn from an exploratory ϵ\epsilonϵ-greedy μ\muμ; the max⁡a′\max_{a'}maxa′​ inside the target implicitly samples the greedy π\piπ instead. You explore with one policy while learning the value of a different, better one.

Why bother with off-policy at all (from L5 notes §7):

  1. Learn from watching someone else act, and conclude you could have done better than they did — not mere imitation.
  2. Reuse old data — GPI walks through π1,π2,…\pi_1,\pi_2,\dotsπ1​,π2​,… and discarding each policy's data is wasteful (this is why DQN-style methods are off-policy).
  3. Explore however you like, while still converging to the deterministic optimal policy.
  4. Ask several counterfactual questions off one single stream of experience.

The non-obvious causal link: off-policy Monte-Carlo needs an importance-sampling correction multiplied across the whole episode,

Gtπ/μ=π(At∣St)μ(At∣St)⋅π(At+1∣St+1)μ(At+1∣St+1)⋯π(AT∣ST)μ(AT∣ST)  Gt,G_t^{\pi/\mu}=\frac{\pi(A_t|S_t)}{\mu(A_t|S_t)}\cdot\frac{\pi(A_{t+1}|S_{t+1})}{\mu(A_{t+1}|S_{t+1})}\cdots\frac{\pi(A_T|S_T)}{\mu(A_T|S_T)}\;G_t, Gtπ/μ​=μ(At​∣St​)π(At​∣St​)​⋅μ(At+1​∣St+1​)π(At+1​∣St+1​)​⋯μ(AT​∣ST​)π(AT​∣ST​)​Gt​,

and this ratio decays toward zero over a long episode — not just "high variance," but unusable. This is what forces off-policy learning to bootstrap: a one-step TD target only needs one importance ratio, because everything past the bootstrap point is already summarized inside VVV or QQQ. Q-learning goes one step further and needs no importance sampling at all, because sampling the Bellman optimality equation (which already contains the max⁡\maxmax) never required an expectation over μ\muμ to begin with — it's sampling a different equation, not correcting a mismatched one.

Bellman equation sampled Converges to
Sarsa expectation equation for qπq_\piqπ​ qπq_\piqπ​
Q-learning optimality equation for q∗q_*q∗​ q∗q_*q∗​

So Sarsa-vs-Q-learning is not a style choice — it's a choice of which Bellman equation you're sampling, and on/off-policy status falls out of that choice mechanically.

Q3. What is GLIE, and what does ϵk=1/k\epsilon_k=1/kϵk​=1/k actually mean?

Asked: "GLIE에서 epsilon=1/k가 된다는게 뭔소린지 설명좀. GLIE가 뭔지 잘 이해 못했어 특히 두번째조건" — 2026-08-25 chat session.

GLIE = Greedy in the Limit with Infinite Exploration. It's the requirement a behaviour schedule (how exploratory episode kkk's policy is) must satisfy for MC control / Sarsa to converge to the true optimum q∗q_*q∗​, not merely to some self-consistent value.

1) lim⁡k→∞Nk(s,a)=∞2) lim⁡k→∞πk(a∣s)=1(a=arg⁡max⁡a′Qk(s,a′))\text{1) } \lim_{k\to\infty}N_k(s,a)=\infty \qquad\qquad \text{2) } \lim_{k\to\infty}\pi_k(a\mid s)=\mathbf{1}\big(a=\arg\max_{a'}Q_k(s,a')\big) 1) k→∞lim​Nk​(s,a)=∞2) k→∞lim​πk​(a∣s)=1(a=arga′max​Qk​(s,a′))

Why condition 2 exists — the part that doesn't just fall out of intuition. The target we actually want to solve is the Bellman optimality equation, which has a max⁡\maxmax inside it:

q∗(s,a)=E[R+γmax⁡a′q∗(s′,a′)]q_*(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\big[R+\gamma\max_{a'}q_*(s',a')\big] q∗​(s,a)=E[R+γa′max​q∗​(s′,a′)]

If the behaviour policy stays soft forever — e.g. a fixed ϵ=0.1\epsilon=0.1ϵ=0.1-greedy policy that never anneals — then the one-step target R+γQ(S′,A′)R+\gamma Q(S',A')R+γQ(S′,A′), with A′A'A′ drawn from that same soft policy, is not sampling the equation above. It is sampling the Bellman expectation equation for that specific soft π\piπ:

qπ(s,a)=E[R+γ∑a′π(a′∣s′)qπ(s′,a′)]q_\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\Big[R+\gamma\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')q_\pi(s',a')\Big] qπ​(s,a)=E[R+γa′∑​π(a′∣s′)qπ​(s′,a′)]

qπq_\piqπ​ (weighted average over actions) and q∗q_*q∗​ (max over actions) are different numbers — a policy that keeps a permanent chance of acting randomly is worth strictly less than the true optimum. Without condition 2, the algorithm converges perfectly — just to the wrong target. The behaviour has to actually become greedy for the sampled equation to turn into the optimality equation instead of a fixed soft policy's expectation equation.

Why ϵk=1/k\epsilon_k=1/kϵk​=1/k satisfies both. Condition 2 is immediate: ϵk→0⇒\epsilon_k\to0 \Rightarrowϵk​→0⇒ greedy-action probability →1\to1→1. Condition 1 is the subtler one — it isn't about whether ϵk\epsilon_kϵk​ shrinks, it's about whether the sum of exploration probability across all episodes still diverges:

∑k=1∞ϵk=∑k=1∞1k=∞(harmonic series)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon_k=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1k=\infty \qquad(\text{harmonic series}) k=1∑∞​ϵk​=k=1∑∞​k1​=∞(harmonic series)

Even though each episode's exploration chance shrinks, the cumulative exploration budget over infinite episodes is still infinite, which is what guarantees every (s,a)(s,a)(s,a) pair keeps getting hit forever rather than being abandoned.

constant ϵ=0.1\epsilon=0.1ϵ=0.1 ∞\infty∞ ✓ ✗ (never reaches 0) qπq_\piqπ​ of the soft policy, not q∗q_*q∗​

1/k1/k1/k sits exactly on the boundary: it decays to zero (condition 2), but not so fast that the sum converges (condition 1). This is the same shape as the Robbins-Monro step-size condition (∑α=∞, ∑α2<∞\sum\alpha=\infty,\ \sum\alpha^2<\infty∑α=∞, ∑α2<∞) seen in Sarsa's convergence conditions and in L4 Q2 — "travel far enough everywhere, but eventually settle." The lecture notes' own caveat — "It is a valid schedule, not a good one" — means other schedules satisfying the same two properties (e.g. 1/k1/\sqrt{k}1/k​) also work in theory; 1/k1/k1/k is just the simplest textbook example, not a claim that it's practically efficient.
